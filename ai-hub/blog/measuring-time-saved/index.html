Measuring time saved without fooling ourselves

Self-reported savings are always generous. Here is the lighter method we use instead, and the numbers it gave us.

ME✦
YOYou
Digital Products
EditMetrics

When we asked 40 colleagues how much time Doc Companion saved them, the average answer was 45 minutes a day. That would add up to almost a full day a week per person. Nobody on the team believed it, including the people who said it.

People are bad at estimating time, and they are especially generous about tools they like. So we stopped asking and started timing.

Time the same task twice

We pick one routine task, such as drafting an appeal outcome letter. Three officers each do six comparable cases: three with the tool and three without, alternating so that tiredness and familiarity even out. Someone sits with them with a stopwatch, or they log start and end times themselves.

Count the review time

A draft that appears in 20 seconds and takes 12 minutes to fix has saved nothing. We record three numbers per case:

  • Time to a first draft
  • Time spent checking and editing
  • Whether a second reviewer had to change anything

The third number matters because it catches drafts that look fine to the author but carry errors a team lead would spot.

Rule of thumb: if checking and editing takes more than half the time the draft saved, fix the prompt or template before rolling the tool out further.

What we found

For appeal letters, the median time fell from 26 minutes to 15. Most of the gain came from not starting with a blank page. Editing time was higher than we expected, about 9 minutes, mostly fixing the reason paragraph. After we changed the template to ask for the reason as bullet points first, editing dropped to 6 minutes.

For meeting notes the gain was larger, roughly 30 minutes per meeting, because the manual version involved replaying parts of the recording.

Why we accept the extra effort

Timing 18 cases takes most of a morning, compared with ten minutes for a survey. But when we presented "11 minutes saved per letter, measured on 18 cases" to the service leadership team, the conversation moved straight to where else we could use it. Nobody questioned the number, because we could show exactly how we got it.

We now do this for every product before it leaves beta, and we publish the method alongside the result.
